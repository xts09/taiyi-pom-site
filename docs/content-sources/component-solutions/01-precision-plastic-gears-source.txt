3. Component Scope
Precision plastic gears include molded transmission elements used to transfer rotary motion, change speed or torque, synchronize mechanisms, and position moving assemblies. Depending on the mechanism, they may operate against another polymer gear, a metal pinion, or a worm. Unlike a general structural molding, a gear must maintain tooth geometry while experiencing repeated bending, sliding, contact stress, frictional heating, and dimensional change. For that reason, material selection cannot be separated from tooth geometry, backlash, shaft alignment, mold shrinkage, operating temperature, and expected duty cycle. [VDI](https://www.vdi.de/en/home/vdi-standards/details/vdi-2736-blatt-2-thermoplastic-gear-wheels-cylindrical-gears-calculation-of-the-load-carrying-capacity?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

4. Engineering Priorities
4.1 Tooth Load and Bending Fatigue
Why it matters:
Each tooth sees a repeating bending load near the root as torque is transmitted. Tooth-root fracture can therefore be controlled by torque, tooth size, face width, stress concentration, temperature, material fatigue behavior, and molding defects rather than tensile strength alone. Thermoplastic gear design standards specifically consider tooth-root breakage together with flank damage, deformation, temperature, and wear. [VDI](https://www.vdi.de/en/home/vdi-standards/details/vdi-2736-blatt-2-thermoplastic-gear-wheels-cylindrical-gears-calculation-of-the-load-carrying-capacity?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Information the customer should provide:
Continuous and peak torque
Gear module, tooth count, and face width
Driver or driven gear position
Required cycle life
Possible validation method:
Instrumented gear endurance test using production-intent geometry, torque, speed, temperature, and duty cycle.
Risks if ignored:
Root cracking, tooth breakage, overload deformation, or apparently random field-life variation.
4.2 Speed, Sliding and Frictional Heat
Why it matters:
Plastic gear wear cannot be judged only from a generic coefficient of friction. Sliding speed, pressure, temperature, surface finish, and geometry interact. Research on acetal gears has shown a transition from comparatively slow wear to rapid wear when operating conditions create excessive tooth-surface temperature. This makes speed and thermal conditions central material-selection variables. [科学直通车](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0301679X0900228X?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Information the customer should provide:
Input and output rpm
Continuous versus intermittent duty
Ambient temperature
Enclosure and cooling conditions
Possible validation method:
Running gear test with tooth/root temperature monitoring and periodic flank-wear measurement.
Risks if ignored:
Accelerated wear, thermal softening, tooth distortion, rising backlash, or premature failure.
4.3 Counterface and Lubrication Condition
Why it matters:
A POM gear running against POM does not necessarily behave like POM running against steel, PA, or another tribological compound. Friction and wear depend on mating material, surface finish, pressure, velocity, temperature, and clearance. Lubrication can also change operating limits, but lubricant compatibility and long-term retention need to be verified. [Delrin](https://www.delrin.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Delrin-Design-Guide-NA-FNL.pdf)
Information the customer should provide:
Mating gear material
Counterface hardness and finish if metal
Dry, grease-lubricated, or oil-lubricated operation
Lubricant specification
Possible validation method:
Paired-gear wear test using the production counterface and lubricant.
Risks if ignored:
Unexpected wear ranking, noise, debris generation, lubricant incompatibility, or excessive counterface wear.
4.4 Backlash, Tooth Accuracy and Dimensional Stability
Why it matters:
Gear performance depends on more than a nominal outside diameter. Pitch, profile, helix, runout, bore position, center distance, and backlash affect meshing. ISO 1328 establishes flank-tolerance systems but cautions that tolerance data for an individual loose gear should not automatically be translated into assembled transmission performance. [ISO](https://www.iso.org/obp/ui/?_escaped_fragment_=iso%3Astd%3Aiso%3A1328%3A-1%3Aed-2%3Av1%3Aen&utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Information the customer should provide:
Gear drawing and tolerance system
Required backlash
Shaft and bearing tolerances
Gearbox center distance
Possible validation method:
Analytical tooth inspection, double-flank rolling measurement, bore/runout measurement, and assembled transmission testing.
Risks if ignored:
Gear whine, binding, transmission error, excessive backlash, uneven flank contact, or inconsistent assembly.
4.5 Temperature, Humidity and Chemical Exposure
Why it matters:
Mechanical properties and dimensions of polymers vary with temperature and environment. POM generally has relatively low water uptake compared with common aliphatic polyamides, but it is still necessary to account for moisture, thermal expansion, post-molding shrinkage, lubricants, cleaners, and chemicals. Creep also depends on stress, temperature, time, and environment. [Delrin](https://www.delrin.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Delrin-Design-Guide-NA-FNL.pdf)
Information the customer should provide:
Minimum and maximum service temperature
Humidity or water exposure
Oils, grease, cleaners, fuels, or chemicals
Storage and operating environment
Possible validation method:
Environmental conditioning followed by dimensional measurement and functional gear testing.
Risks if ignored:
Backlash shift, tight mesh, loss of stiffness, creep, chemical degradation, or changing gear noise.
4.6 Duty Cycle, Life and Noise Requirements
Why it matters:
A gear used for occasional positioning is a different material problem from one rotating continuously for thousands of hours. Reversing loads, startup torque, impact, stall events, speed cycling, and noise requirements can change both the dominant failure mode and the preferred compound direction. A datasheet cannot reproduce these system-level conditions.
Information the customer should provide:
On/off cycle
Reversing frequency
Startup, stall, or shock loads
Service-life and noise targets
Possible validation method:
Accelerated but representative gearbox endurance and acoustic testing.
Risks if ignored:
A material can pass short mechanical tests yet fail the actual gearbox life or noise requirement.

6. Candidate Material Directions
6.1 Unfilled POM
When it may be considered:
General precision gears where dimensional control, fatigue resistance, stiffness, moldability, and favorable sliding behavior are required without a special reinforcement requirement.
Potential advantages:
A useful balance of mechanical properties, relatively low moisture uptake, dimensional stability, and friction/wear behavior. POM is widely established as a gear material. [巴斯夫塑料与橡胶](https://plastics-rubber.basf.com/northamerica/en/performance_polymers/products/ultraform?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Important tradeoffs:
Temperature, tooth load, and frictional heat still limit performance. An unfilled grade does not automatically satisfy high-load or high-temperature service.
Required project validation:
Gear endurance, tooth temperature, dimensional accuracy, noise, and counterface wear.
Situations where it may not be appropriate:
Very high thermal load, severe tooth deflection, unusual chemical exposure, or applications requiring electrical conductivity.
6.2 Wear-Resistant or Low-Friction POM
When it may be considered:
Dry-running gear sets, higher cycle counts, stringent noise requirements, difficult polymer/metal interfaces, or applications where standard POM shows unacceptable flank wear.
Potential advantages:
Tribological modification may reduce friction, stick-slip, wear, or generated heat under specific mating conditions.
Important tradeoffs:
A lower friction result from one standardized test does not prove longer gear life. Additives can change stiffness, strength, shrinkage, surface behavior, and the interaction with the counterface. Actual wear ranking is condition-dependent. [Delrin](https://www.delrin.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Delrin-Design-Guide-NA-FNL.pdf)
Required project validation:
Production-like paired-gear testing at target torque, rpm, lubrication, and temperature.
Situations where it may not be appropriate:
When failure is primarily tooth bending, alignment, tooling accuracy, or overload rather than tribological wear.
6.3 Reinforced POM
When it may be considered:
When tooth deflection, hub stiffness, creep, or dimensional retention under mechanical load is more limiting than surface wear.
Potential advantages:
Reinforcement can increase stiffness and improve resistance to load-induced deformation.
Important tradeoffs:
Glass fiber creates flow-dependent anisotropy. Mold shrinkage differs with fiber orientation, and warpage can become more difficult to control. Reinforcement can also change tooth-surface behavior and mating-part wear. [Delrin](https://www.delrin.com/delrin-acetal-homopolymer-thermoplastic-resin-molding-guide/)
Required project validation:
Molding simulation or cavity trials, tooth geometry measurements, gear endurance, and counterface inspection.
Situations where it may not be appropriate:
Fine gears where isotropic shrinkage, surface quality, low mating-part wear, or very tight molded accuracy dominates.
6.4 Conductive or Antistatic Compounds
When it may be considered:
Only when the gear or geartrain has a genuine electrostatic-discharge or charge-dissipation requirement.
Potential advantages:
An electrically modified compound may reduce static-charge accumulation while retaining a moldable engineering-plastic platform.
Important tradeoffs:
Conductive fillers can alter mechanical properties, fatigue behavior, friction, wear, shrinkage, appearance, and processing. Conductivity requirements must also be defined quantitatively rather than simply calling the part “antistatic.”
Required project validation:
Electrical-resistance testing together with mechanical, dimensional, wear, and gear-life testing.
Situations where it may not be appropriate:
Normal gearboxes with no ESD or conductivity requirement.
6.5 PA6, PA66 or PPA
When it may be considered:
When the application requires a different balance of toughness, wear behavior, elevated-temperature performance, or chemical resistance than the available POM directions can provide.
Potential advantages:
PA6 and PA66 can be viable gear materials in suitable conditions. PPA may be worth evaluating where greater retention of stiffness at elevated temperature and reduced moisture sensitivity relative to standard aliphatic polyamides are important. [科学直通车](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0043164809001501?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Important tradeoffs:
PA6 and PA66 are more sensitive to moisture conditioning than POM, which matters for gear geometry and backlash. PPA generally brings higher material and processing requirements.
Required project validation:
Conditioned dimensions, hot/wet properties, fatigue, wear, gear noise, molding behavior, and economics.
Situations where it may not be appropriate:
Where POM already meets the mechanical and environmental requirement with simpler dimensional control.
7. Design, Tooling, and Processing Considerations
7.1 Hub, Web and Tooth-Section Balance
A precision gear is not simply a toothed disk. Large differences between hub thickness, web thickness, ribs, and the toothed rim can produce nonuniform cooling and shrinkage. These effects can appear as dish, eccentricity, tooth-profile deviation, or bore movement. Geometry should therefore be reviewed as a complete molded structure, not only by checking tooth strength.
7.2 Gate and Weld-Line Position
Flow should be considered relative to the hub, web, and gear teeth. Gate arrangement can determine where flow fronts meet, and recent testing of molded polyacetal gears found measurable differences between teeth associated with gate and weld-line regions, including different crack-initiation behavior. Gate location should therefore be assessed before tooling is frozen. [J-Stage](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jamdsm/20/3/20_2026jamdsm0034/_article/-char/en)
7.3 Shrinkage Compensation in the Gear Cavity
Plastic gear mold geometry cannot simply equal the required final gear geometry. Cooling and crystallization change the final dimensions, and shrinkage may not be perfectly uniform across a small gear. Published work on injection-molded gears identifies shrinkage compensation as a fundamental challenge in achieving final tooth geometry. [National Cheng Kung University](https://researchoutput.ncku.edu.tw/en/publications/gear-module-adjustment-method-for-shrinkage-compensation-of-injec/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
7.4 Mold Temperature, Packing and Long-Term Dimensions
For semi-crystalline POM, mold temperature and packing conditions influence as-molded and post-molding dimensions. A processing condition that gives an acceptable part immediately after molding may not give the same dimensions after thermal exposure. Production settings should therefore be developed around the actual tolerance and service-temperature requirement rather than cycle time alone. [Delrin](https://www.delrin.com/delrin-acetal-homopolymer-thermoplastic-resin-molding-guide/)
7.5 Fiber Orientation for Reinforced Compounds
If glass-fiber-reinforced POM is evaluated, the gear must be treated as an anisotropic molded structure. Fiber orientation changes shrinkage parallel and transverse to flow and can contribute to distortion. Flow analysis, gate strategy, and cavity-specific measurements become substantially more important when a reinforced material replaces an unfilled material. [Delrin](https://www.delrin.com/delrin-acetal-homopolymer-thermoplastic-resin-molding-guide/)
7.6 Bore, Shaft and Press-Fit Design
A metal shaft, knurl, D-flat, spline, press fit, or insert can introduce substantial local stress into the hub. Excessive interference can cause stress relaxation, creep, hub splitting, or distortion that changes gear runout. The bore interface should therefore be validated together with the gear rather than treated as a separate assembly detail.
7.7 Tooth Surface, Parting Line and Multi-Cavity Consistency
Flash, mismatch, ejection damage, or local surface defects on an active flank can affect noise and wear even when the overall gear diameter is within tolerance. Multi-cavity tooling should be evaluated cavity by cavity for profile, runout, bore position, and functional rolling behavior rather than relying only on overall dimensional sampling.

9. Frequently Asked Questions
1. Is POM a good material for precision plastic gears?
POM is a reasonable candidate for many molded gears because it combines stiffness, fatigue capability, dimensional stability, relatively low moisture uptake, and favorable friction and wear behavior. However, POM should not be selected from the material name alone. Gear geometry, torque, rpm, tooth temperature, mating material, lubrication, environment, and life requirement determine whether standard POM, modified POM, or another polymer direction deserves validation. [巴斯夫塑料与橡胶](https://plastics-rubber.basf.com/northamerica/en/performance_polymers/products/ultraform?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
2. When should a low-friction or wear-resistant POM be considered?
Consider tribologically modified POM when flank wear, frictional heating, stick-slip, dry-running performance, or noise is a demonstrated limitation. Do not use it automatically for every gear. Published POM guidance notes that friction depends on pressure, velocity, temperature, clearance, and mating surface, so a material that performs well in one wear test may rank differently in a real gearbox. Gear-pair testing remains necessary. [Delrin](https://www.delrin.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Delrin-Design-Guide-NA-FNL.pdf)
3. Is glass-fiber-reinforced POM better for high-load gears?
Not necessarily. Glass reinforcement can raise stiffness and reduce tooth or hub deformation, which may help some load-limited designs. It also introduces fiber orientation, anisotropic shrinkage, and greater warpage sensitivity and may change counterface wear. If the existing problem is flank wear, poor alignment, noise, or molding accuracy, simply adding glass fiber can address the wrong failure mechanism. [Delrin](https://www.delrin.com/delrin-acetal-homopolymer-thermoplastic-resin-molding-guide/)
4. POM or PA6/PA66: which is better for gears?
Neither is universally better. POM often offers an attractive combination of dimensional stability and low moisture sensitivity for precision gearing. PA6 or PA66 may offer useful toughness or tribological characteristics in other designs, but their moisture-conditioned dimensions and mechanical properties require careful consideration. PPA may become relevant at more demanding temperatures or humidity conditions, but it also requires separate processing, performance, and cost validation. [科学直通车](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0043164809001501?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
5. How tight can molded POM gear tolerances be?
There is no defensible universal tolerance that applies to every POM gear. Achievable precision depends on gear size, geometry, resin, mold construction, cavity count, gate, cooling, process control, and measurement method. ISO 1328 provides systems for gear-flank tolerance classification, while molded-part guidance emphasizes the influence of resin, mold, molding conditions, and quality control. The tolerance should therefore be agreed around the actual gear and manufacturing process. [ISO](https://www.iso.org/obp/ui/?_escaped_fragment_=iso%3Astd%3Aiso%3A1328%3A-1%3Aed-2%3Av1%3Aen&utm_source=chatgpt.com)
6. Can changing the material solve gear noise?
Sometimes, but material is only one variable. Noise may originate from flank wear or friction, but it can also come from pitch or profile error, runout, incorrect backlash, shaft misalignment, bearing clearance, housing deformation, motor excitation, or inconsistent molding. Before changing resin, measure the gear geometry and assembled transmission. A tribological compound is unlikely to fix a gearbox whose center distance or tooth geometry is incorrect.
7. What testing should be done before approving a new gear compound?
Start with material data, but finish with the real component. A useful validation program normally includes dimensional inspection, bore/runout and tooth checks, conditioned measurements if temperature or humidity matters, and running tests using the real mating gear, torque, rpm, lubricant, duty cycle, and representative environment. Wear, temperature, noise, backlash growth, and failure location should be tracked rather than relying only on tensile properties.

Precision Plastic Gears: Material and Application Considerations
Gear Material Solutions
Precision plastic gears operate under repeated tooth loading, sliding contact, dimensional constraints, and often demanding noise or service-life requirements. PLATFORM modified POM compounds can be evaluated as candidate material directions where wear behavior, stiffness, dimensional stability, or friction need to be balanced.
Final material selection should be based on the actual gear geometry, operating temperature, counterface, lubrication condition, duty cycle, and validation results.
Material Selection Starts with the Gear System
Precision plastic gears include spur gears, helical gears, pinions, planet gears, sun gears, internal gears, compound gears, worm wheels, and sector gears. They are used in compact gearboxes, actuators, appliance drives, office equipment, motion systems, and other electromechanical assemblies.
Unlike a general structural molding, a gear experiences repeated tooth-root bending and sliding contact while its tooth geometry must remain within a functional tolerance window. Temperature, torque, speed, mating material, lubrication, backlash, and mold shrinkage can therefore be just as important as the basic strength of the polymer. Thermoplastic gear design practice specifically considers root failure, flank damage, tooth deformation, temperature, and wear. [VDI](https://www.vdi.de/en/home/vdi-standards/details/vdi-2736-blatt-2-thermoplastic-gear-wheels-cylindrical-gears-calculation-of-the-load-carrying-capacity?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Key Engineering Questions
Torque and Tooth Loading
Continuous torque is only part of the load case. Startup torque, stall conditions, reversing motion, shock loads, tooth count, module, and face width influence tooth-root stress and deformation.
If a gear is already breaking, the fracture location should be examined before changing material. A stronger or reinforced compound will not correct inadequate tooth geometry, shaft misalignment, or repeated overload.
Speed and Frictional Heat
Higher rotational speed increases the importance of sliding behavior and heat generation at the tooth interface. Research on acetal gears has shown that wear can change sharply when operating conditions raise gear-surface temperature beyond an acceptable region. [科学直通车](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0043164809001501?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
For continuously rotating gears, provide rpm, transmitted torque, ambient temperature, enclosure conditions, and duty cycle. These values are necessary to distinguish a modest wear problem from a thermal limitation.
Counterface and Lubrication
A plastic gear does not operate by itself. The mating gear may be POM, another engineering polymer, steel, or another metal, and each pairing creates a different tribological system.
Surface finish, pressure, speed, clearance, temperature, and lubricant all influence friction and wear. For this reason, material screening should eventually be followed by testing with the actual mating gear and production lubricant. [Delrin](https://www.delrin.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Delrin-Design-Guide-NA-FNL.pdf)
Backlash and Gear Accuracy
A gear can meet a basic diameter tolerance and still perform poorly. Pitch deviation, tooth profile, helix, runout, bore concentricity, center distance, shaft position, and housing tolerances affect meshing and noise.
ISO gear standards provide established methods for classifying tooth-flank deviations, but an individual loose-gear tolerance does not by itself guarantee assembled gearbox performance. Functional rolling and transmission testing may therefore be required. [ISO](https://www.iso.org/obp/ui/?_escaped_fragment_=iso%3Astd%3Aiso%3A1328%3A-1%3Aed-2%3Av1%3Aen&utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Common Gear Problems
Rapid Tooth Wear
Visible polishing, flank loss, wear debris, increasing backlash, or progressive noise can indicate a tribological problem. Potential causes include excessive load or speed, high tooth temperature, an unsuitable counterface, poor alignment, rough mating surfaces, or insufficient lubrication.
A wear-resistant compound may be worth evaluating, but only after these operating conditions are understood.
Tooth Breakage
A broken tooth may result from fatigue, insufficient stiffness, impact, overload, root geometry, molding defects, or a local weld-line condition. The solution is not necessarily a higher-strength resin.
The fracture surface, crack origin, load history, mold flow, gate location, and tooth geometry should be examined first.
Noise or Irregular Motion
Gear noise can originate from friction, but it can also result from pitch error, runout, incorrect backlash, center-distance variation, shaft alignment, bearings, or gearbox deformation.
Changing to a low-friction material without identifying the source of transmission error can leave the underlying problem unchanged.
Tight Mesh After Temperature or Environmental Change
Polymers change dimensions with temperature and environment. POM has relatively low moisture uptake, but thermal expansion, post-molding shrinkage, creep, and environmental exposure still have to be included in the tolerance stack. [Delrin](https://www.delrin.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Delrin-Design-Guide-NA-FNL.pdf)
Measure the complete assembly at relevant hot, cold, and conditioned states when clearance is critical.
Candidate Material Directions
Unfilled POM
Unfilled POM is often a useful starting direction for precision molded gears that require a combination of stiffness, fatigue capability, dimensional stability, moldability, and favorable sliding characteristics.
It should still be validated for the actual torque, speed, service temperature, lubrication, and required life. A standard material can be unsuitable when frictional heating, tooth deflection, or environmental exposure becomes severe.
Wear-Resistant or Low-Friction POM
A tribologically modified POM may be considered when dry running, flank wear, stick-slip, frictional heating, or noise has been identified as a limiting factor.
The correct direction depends on the mating material and operating conditions. A lower coefficient of friction measured in a generic material test does not automatically translate into a longer-lived gear.
Reinforced POM
Reinforced POM may be considered when increased stiffness or reduced load-induced deformation is required. It can be relevant for gears where tooth deflection, hub movement, or creep is more critical than surface wear.
The tradeoff is more complex molding behavior. Fiber orientation creates directional shrinkage and can increase sensitivity to warpage, gate position, and flow pattern. [Delrin](https://www.delrin.com/delrin-acetal-homopolymer-thermoplastic-resin-molding-guide/)
For fine-pitch or highly accuracy-sensitive gears, this dimensional tradeoff requires careful evaluation.
Conductive or Antistatic Compounds
Conductive or antistatic materials should only enter the material-selection process when the gear or surrounding system has a defined electrical requirement.
Conductive fillers can also change stiffness, shrinkage, fatigue, friction, wear, and surface behavior. Electrical performance and gear performance therefore need to be validated together.
When PA6, PA66, or PPA May Be Considered
POM should not be forced into an application whose requirements favor another polymer family.
PA6 or PA66 may be considered when a different balance of toughness or wear behavior is required, but moisture conditioning can have a greater influence on dimensions and properties. PPA may become relevant where elevated-temperature mechanics and dimensional stability under humid conditions justify a higher-performance material direction. [巴斯夫塑料与橡胶](https://plastics-rubber.basf.com/northamerica/en/performance_polymers/fpgs/polyphthalamides?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
These alternatives require their own molding, conditioning, dimensional, wear, and cost validation.
Gear Design and Injection Molding Matter
Control Section Thickness
Hub, web, rib, and rim geometry should be designed as a single molded structure. Large changes in section thickness can create uneven cooling and shrinkage, which may appear as dish, eccentricity, bore displacement, or tooth deviation.
Review Gate and Weld-Line Position
Gate location determines the melt-flow pattern through the gear. Where flow fronts meet, weld-line regions can have different local structure and properties.
Recent research on injection-molded POM gears found differences in tooth helix deviation and root-crack behavior associated with weld-line regions in the tested gear configuration. Gate strategy should therefore be reviewed before the mold design is finalized. [J-Stage](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jamdsm/20/3/20_2026jamdsm0034/_article/-char/en)
Compensate for Molding Shrinkage
The mold cavity cannot simply duplicate the required finished gear dimensions. Semi-crystalline polymers shrink during cooling, and gear shrinkage may not be perfectly uniform.
Published studies on small injection-molded plastic gears identify nonlinear shrinkage as a significant dimensional-accuracy problem. Tool compensation should therefore be based on the selected material, geometry, process, and measured trial parts. [PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8271480/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Control the Bore and Shaft Interface
Press fits, D-shafts, splines, knurls, and metal inserts can introduce substantial stress into the hub. Excessive interference may produce creep, relaxation, splitting, or runout changes.
Provide the shaft material, nominal interference, temperature range, and assembly method when requesting material evaluation.
Validate Multi-Cavity Production
A precision gear should not be approved solely from a few nominal dimensions. For multi-cavity tooling, profile, pitch, runout, bore position, and functional rolling behavior should be reviewed by cavity.
Process consistency matters because a gearbox may amplify small differences that would be insignificant in a non-moving plastic component.

Frequently Asked Questions
Is POM suitable for precision gears?
POM is a credible candidate for many precision gear applications because of its combination of stiffness, fatigue capability, relatively low moisture uptake, dimensional stability, and sliding behavior. It is not a universal answer. Torque, speed, temperature, counterface, lubrication, geometry, and service life must still be validated.
Should every plastic gear use a wear-resistant POM?
No. A wear-modified material is useful when wear or friction is actually limiting performance.
If the failure is caused by tooth bending, incorrect backlash, poor mold accuracy, misalignment, or overload, changing the tribological package may add cost without addressing the dominant mechanism.
Is glass-filled POM automatically stronger for gears?
It is generally stiffer, but that does not make it automatically better.
Fiber orientation changes shrinkage and warpage behavior, and reinforcement can affect the mating surface. It is most relevant when structural stiffness is the actual limitation and the resulting molding anisotropy can be controlled.
How should a new gear material be approved?
Material-property data should be used for screening, not final approval. Production-intent gears should be checked dimensionally and then run with the actual mating gear at representative torque, rpm, temperature, lubrication, and duty cycle.
Track wear, temperature, noise, backlash change, and failure location over the required test period.
