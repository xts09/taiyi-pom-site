3. Component Scope
Conveyor chain components include molded links and attachments that form articulated conveying systems for bottles, containers, workpieces, packaged goods, and industrial components. Depending on the design, a chain may run straight, articulate through horizontal curves, engage sprockets, carry attachments, or slide continuously against wear strips. Each link therefore combines structural and tribological functions: it must transmit chain tension while maintaining hinge geometry and controlled sliding contact. Material selection should be based on chain pull, speed, pin and guide materials, curve loading, contamination, temperature, lubrication, and cleaning chemistry rather than nominal tensile strength alone. 4. Engineering Priorities
4.1 Chain Pull and Tensile Loading
Why it matters:
Every loaded link must transmit the tensile force generated by the conveyed load, sliding resistance, elevation changes, acceleration, accumulation, and conveyor geometry. Excessive chain pull can deform hinge regions, overload attachments, or increase pressure at the pin joint and guide interfaces. Temperature also changes the usable mechanical performance of a polymer, so nominal room-temperature strength alone is not an adequate selection criterion.
Information the customer should provide:
Conveyed product weight and total loaded length
Conveyor layout and incline/decline
Chain speed
Startup, stop, accumulation, and jam conditions
Possible validation method:
Conveyor force calculation followed by tensile, creep, and full-chain endurance testing at representative temperature.
Risks if ignored:
Permanent elongation, hinge deformation, fractured links, attachment failure, or premature chain replacement.
4.2 Hinge, Pin, and Bore Wear
Why it matters:
Each articulated chain joint behaves as a repeatedly moving bearing interface. Relative motion between pin and hinge/bore surfaces gradually increases joint clearance, producing apparent chain elongation and progressively changing the relationship between chain pitch and sprocket engagement. Chain-industry technical guidance identifies pin/joint wear as a major source of chain elongation. [iwis](https://www.iwis.com/en-en/products-services/maintenance/lubrication-maintenance?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Information the customer should provide:
Pin material and diameter
Chain pitch and hinge geometry
Articulation frequency
Lubrication condition
Possible validation method:
Articulating-chain endurance test with periodic pitch and hinge-clearance measurement.
Risks if ignored:
Pitch growth, poor sprocket engagement, chain jumping, vibration, noise, and accelerated sprocket wear.
4.3 Sliding Friction Against Wear Strips and Corner Tracks
Why it matters:
The underside and side-thrust surfaces of many plastic chains slide continuously against polymer guide tracks. Friction therefore contributes directly to conveyor drive load and interface temperature. In side-flexing conveyors, curved tracks introduce additional lateral contact. Industrial chain manuals specifically associate high speed, rough tracks, poor lubrication, embedded abrasive material, and tight spots with plastic wear debris and abnormal wear. [Rexnord](https://www.rexnord.com/ContentItems/TechLibrary/Documents/8rxTTmm-en_Manual)
Information the customer should provide:
Wear-strip and corner-track material
Straight and curved conveyor lengths
Chain speed
Dry or lubricated operation
Possible validation method:
Material-pair friction/wear screening plus production conveyor endurance testing.
Risks if ignored:
Higher drive load, heat generation, dusting, rapid underside wear, side-thrust wear, or chain binding.
4.4 Impact, Accumulation, and Jam Loading
Why it matters:
A conveyor link may experience loads far above normal steady-state conveying when containers accumulate, workpieces impact flights, products jam, or a chain suddenly restarts. These events can damage tabs, attachments, hinge regions, or top plates. Conveyor troubleshooting literature lists obstructions, tight curves, chemical attack, impact loading, and inadequate support among causes of broken chain features. [Rexnord](https://www.rexnord.com/ContentItems/TechLibrary/Documents/8rxTTmm-en_Manual)
Information the customer should provide:
Product mass and spacing
Accumulation conditions
Stop/start frequency
Known jam or impact events
Possible validation method:
Impact testing and conveyor-cycle testing including controlled accumulation and restart events.
Risks if ignored:
Broken flights, cracked tabs, distorted hinges, unexpected overload failure, or conveyor downtime.
4.5 Temperature, Water, and Cleaning Chemicals
Why it matters:
Polymer chain behavior depends strongly on both temperature and environment. Commercial chain engineering data distinguish between dry and wet service and use different material families where hot water, oxidizing chemicals, acids, or abrasive conditions are involved. POM should therefore not be assumed compatible with every washdown or sanitation chemistry simply because it performs well in ordinary industrial environments. [Rexnord](https://www.rexnord.com/ContentItems/TechLibrary/Documents/8rxTTmm-en_Manual)
Information the customer should provide:
Continuous and peak operating temperature
Wet or dry operation
Cleaner and sanitizer chemistry
Concentration, exposure time, and cleaning frequency
Possible validation method:
ISO 175 chemical-immersion screening followed by stressed component and conveyor testing. [ISO](https://www.iso.org/standard/55483.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Risks if ignored:
Embrittlement, swelling, surface degradation, cracking, dimensional change, or loss of chain strength.
4.6 Lubrication, Debris, and Cleanliness
Why it matters:
Lubrication can reduce friction, chain tension, and wear, but some production lines intentionally operate dry to simplify hygiene or avoid product contamination. Dry operation makes guide finish, material pairing, and debris management more important. Conveyor maintenance guidance also shows that dirt, spilled product, and other deposits can increase wear, power demand, and chain pulsation. [Rexnord](https://www.rexnord.com/ContentItems/TechLibrary/Documents/8rxTTmm-en_Manual)
Information the customer should provide:
Dry or lubricated conveyor
Lubricant specification
Dust, fibers, glass, metal chips, or product residue
Cleaning procedure
Possible validation method:
Clean and contaminated conveyor endurance testing with representative maintenance cycles.
Risks if ignored:
Abrasive wear, stick-slip, drive-current increase, plastic dust, contaminated lubricant, or unstable service life.
4.7 Pitch, Sprocket, and Guide Compatibility
Why it matters:
A chain is geometrically coupled to its sprockets and guides. Pitch error, hinge deformation, inconsistent link dimensions, incorrect shaft position, or guide interference can disrupt articulation and sprocket entry. ISO 1977 emphasizes correct chain/sprocket dimensional relationships for normal meshing and load transmission, while plastic-chain maintenance guidance stresses correct wear-strip and sprocket positioning. [ISO](https://www.iso.org/cms/%20render/live/en/sites/isoorg/contents/data/standard/04/39/43971.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Information the customer should provide:
Chain pitch and sprocket geometry
Existing chain drawing
Guide-track layout and clearances
Critical cumulative dimensions
Possible validation method:
Pitch inspection, assembled articulation checks, sprocket engagement testing, and conveyor trials.
Risks if ignored:
Chain jumping, pulsation, binding, local wear, noise, or accelerated sprocket damage.

6. Candidate Material Directions
6.1 Unfilled POM
When it may be considered:
Standard molded chain links operating under moderate load and speed where dimensional stability, strength, moldability, and favorable sliding behavior are required.
Potential advantages:
POM is already widely used as a base material for polymer conveyor chains. Commercial chain-material guides describe standard POM as combining useful strength and friction characteristics for conventional conveying conditions. [Habasit](https://uattsthbst.habasit.com/-/media/Project/Habasit/PublicWebSite/Documents/English/Products/HabaCHAIN/web-4185-en_0416-new-layout.pdf)
Important tradeoffs:
Standard POM does not automatically solve high-speed dry running, abrasive contamination, aggressive chemistry, or elevated-temperature load retention.
Required project validation:
Chain pull, creep, hinge wear, track wear, sprocket engagement, temperature, and service-life testing.
Situations where it may not be appropriate:
Severe chemical exposure, substantial abrasive contamination, extreme thermal duty, or defined ESD requirements.
6.2 Wear-Resistant or Low-Friction POM
When it may be considered:
High-cycle conveyors, dry-running systems, higher-speed lines, side-flexing chains, or applications showing excessive chain/track friction or wear.
Potential advantages:
Commercial conveyor systems use low-friction POM formulations specifically to reduce friction and improve wear behavior in demanding conveying conditions. [Habasit](https://tdm.habasit.com/PDS/en-us/Plastics%20Materials/HabasitLINK%20POM_LF%2C%20low%20friction%20Polyoxymethylene%20%28Acetal%29-en-us.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Important tradeoffs:
A lower material friction value does not guarantee lower chain wear or lower drive load in every system. The wear-strip material, contamination, chain pull, curvature, and surface finish remain decisive.
Required project validation:
Production-intent chain against the actual wear strip and corner track at representative speed and load.
Situations where it may not be appropriate:
Failures dominated by overload, poor sprocket geometry, guide misalignment, chemical attack, or impact rather than tribology.
6.3 Reinforced POM
When it may be considered:
Projects where stiffness, structural deformation, creep, or link-load retention is limiting and the resulting tribological and molding effects can be controlled.
Potential advantages:
Reinforcement can increase stiffness and may reduce deformation of structurally loaded features.
Important tradeoffs:
Fiber reinforcement introduces directional shrinkage and can change surface wear against pins, sprockets, and guides. It may also reduce the design margin against impact in certain geometries. ISO 294-4 distinguishes shrinkage parallel and transverse to melt flow, which becomes particularly relevant for filled systems. [ISO](https://www.iso.org/standard/70413.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Required project validation:
Mold-flow orientation, pitch accuracy, hinge strength, impact, counterface wear, and full-chain endurance.
Situations where it may not be appropriate:
Thin hinge features, highly impact-loaded links, soft guide materials, or geometries requiring very isotropic shrinkage.
6.4 Conductive or Antistatic Compounds
When it may be considered:
Electronics handling, sensitive component conveying, or other systems with a defined charge-control requirement.
Potential advantages:
Conductive or dissipative compounds can create a controlled electrical path when integrated into an appropriately grounded conveyor system. Commercial plastic chains already demonstrate that conductive POM is technically possible. [Habasit](https://uattsthbst.habasit.com/-/media/Project/Habasit/PublicWebSite/Documents/English/Products/HabaCHAIN/web-4185-en_0416-new-layout.pdf)
Important tradeoffs:
A conductive chain alone does not create a complete ESD-control system. Guides, wear strips, machine frame, grounding, contamination, humidity, and electrical continuity must be considered.
Required project validation:
Resistance/resistivity measurement using an applicable method such as IEC 61340-2-3, plus wear and mechanical testing. [IEC Webstore](https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/25218?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Situations where it may not be appropriate:
Conveyors without a quantified static-control requirement.
6.5 PA6, PA66, or PPA
When it may be considered:
PA6 or PA66 may be evaluated where dry abrasive service, impact behavior, or a different mechanical balance is needed. PPA may warrant project-specific evaluation when elevated-temperature stiffness or creep retention is more important than the conventional POM performance envelope.
Potential advantages:
Polyamide-based conveyor-chain materials are used commercially for selected abrasive, high-load, or elevated-temperature dry applications. [Habasit](https://uattsthbst.habasit.com/-/media/Project/Habasit/PublicWebSite/Documents/English/Products/HabaCHAIN/web-4185-en_0416-new-layout.pdf)
Important tradeoffs:
PA6 and PA66 absorb considerably more moisture than POM, affecting dimensions and properties. For chains, this can alter pitch, hinge clearance, and guide fit. PPA reduces some moisture-related limitations but does not remove the need for wear, cost, and processing validation.
Required project validation:
Conditioned dimensions, tensile/creep behavior, hinge wear, chemical exposure, temperature, and sprocket engagement.
Situations where it may not be appropriate:
Wet applications requiring particularly stable molded dimensions where POM already satisfies mechanical and environmental requirements.
7. Design, Tooling, and Processing Considerations
7.1 Protect the Hinge and Pin-Bore Geometry
The hinge region carries load while repeatedly articulating around the chain pin. Bore roundness, barrel thickness, pin clearance, and alignment between hinge sections therefore influence both strength and wear. Gate or ejector decisions should not introduce obvious weakness or distortion into these highly loaded features.
7.2 Keep Weld Lines Away from Critical Load Paths
Chain links often contain multiple hinge barrels, tabs, ribs, and apertures, so melt fronts may divide and reconnect. A weld line located through a thin attachment root or highly stressed hinge section deserves specific evaluation. Gate number and position should be reviewed using the load path rather than molding convenience alone.
7.3 Control Pitch Through Shrinkage and Tool Compensation
Individual link dimensions appear small, but pitch errors accumulate over many links and ultimately affect sprocket engagement. ISO 294-4 recognizes both molding and post-molding shrinkage, while ISO 20457 establishes manufacturing-tolerance principles for molded plastic parts. Tool compensation should therefore be based on production material and measured molded parts rather than a single generic shrinkage value. [ISO](https://www.iso.org/standard/70413.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
7.4 Control Flatness and Sliding Surfaces
A warped chain link can create localized contact on wear strips or interfere with neighboring links. Top surfaces may also need to remain sufficiently flat for stable product conveyance. Uneven wall thickness, asymmetric ribs, packing differences, and cooling imbalance should be reviewed against both sliding function and conveyed-product stability.
7.5 Treat Fiber Orientation Carefully
If reinforced POM is evaluated, melt flow determines fiber orientation and therefore directional shrinkage. The orientation around hinge barrels, thin webs, guide tabs, and attachment roots can influence both mechanical properties and warpage. A reinforced formulation should not simply be substituted into tooling developed around unfilled POM without dimensional validation. [ISO](https://www.iso.org/standard/70413.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
7.6 Avoid Flash and Sharp Edges at Articulating Interfaces
Flash around hinge bores or sliding surfaces can increase friction, restrict articulation, or generate wear debris. Sharp molded edges can also scrape adjacent links or guide tracks. Parting-line location, venting, tool wear, and trimming strategy therefore become functional rather than cosmetic issues.
7.7 Validate Multi-Cavity Consistency
High-volume chain links are often molded in multi-cavity tooling. Small cavity-to-cavity differences in pitch, bore size, tab thickness, flatness, or shrinkage can accumulate when many links are assembled into one chain. Critical dimensions should be tracked by cavity, and functional chain sections should be assembled from representative production parts.

9. Frequently Asked Questions
1. Is POM suitable for plastic conveyor chain links?
POM is a credible starting material for many molded conveyor-chain components and is already used commercially in standard and low-friction chain systems. Its combination of stiffness, dimensional stability, strength, and sliding behavior makes it technically relevant. However, suitability depends on chain pull, speed, hinge design, pin material, wear-strip pairing, temperature, chemical exposure, and expected life. A material-family label cannot replace conveyor-system validation. [Habasit](https://uattsthbst.habasit.com/-/media/Project/Habasit/PublicWebSite/Documents/English/Products/HabaCHAIN/web-4185-en_0416-new-layout.pdf)
2. When is low-friction POM worth considering?
It becomes relevant when dry-running friction, drive load, underside wear, side-thrust wear, or plastic dust has been identified as a real limitation. The wear-strip and corner-track material must be included in the evaluation. Conveyor manuals show that rough tracks, embedded abrasives, poor lubrication, high speed, and tight spots can all create wear debris, so changing the chain compound without correcting those conditions may deliver little improvement. [Rexnord](https://www.rexnord.com/ContentItems/TechLibrary/Documents/8rxTTmm-en_Manual)
3. Why does a plastic conveyor chain become longer during service?
The first suspect is often wear at the articulated pin/hinge interface rather than uniform stretching of the entire link. As each joint develops additional clearance, the accumulated pitch of a long chain increases. Creep or permanent deformation from excessive tensile load can also contribute. Measure several pitches, inspect pins and bores, and check chain pull and sprocket condition before assuming that the base polymer is insufficient. [iwis](https://www.iwis.com/en-us/technical-center/testing-analysis/wear-resistance?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
4. Can reinforced POM carry more load than unfilled POM?
Potentially, but that is not enough to justify the change. Reinforcement can increase stiffness, yet it also creates anisotropic shrinkage and can alter hinge impact behavior and wear against pins or guide tracks. For a chain link, pitch control and joint durability can be more important than a higher tensile modulus. Reinforced POM should therefore be treated as a separate candidate requiring new dimensional, wear, impact, and endurance validation.
5. Should we use POM or PA for an abrasive conveyor?
There is no universal winner. POM is commonly attractive for dimensional stability and sliding performance. PA6 or PA66 may be considered for selected dry abrasive or mechanically demanding applications, and commercial conveyor-chain portfolios use specialized PA materials for some such conditions. However, polyamide moisture uptake can change dimensions and properties. The correct comparison requires the actual abrasive, humidity, temperature, guides, pins, and operating speed. [Habasit](https://uattsthbst.habasit.com/-/media/Project/Habasit/PublicWebSite/Documents/English/Products/HabaCHAIN/web-4185-en_0416-new-layout.pdf)
6. How tightly should conveyor-chain pitch be controlled?
There is no single tolerance appropriate for every molded chain design. Individual link pitch, hinge clearance, molding shrinkage, multi-cavity variation, and post-molding dimensional change all contribute to cumulative chain geometry. The relevant requirement should be derived from the chain/sprocket system. ISO 20457 provides a framework for molded-plastic tolerances, while actual sprocket engagement must be validated on assembled chain sections. [ISO](https://www.iso.org/cms/%20render/live/en/sites/isoorg/contents/data/standard/06/80/68097.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
7. Does an antistatic or conductive chain solve an ESD problem by itself?
No. Charge control requires a continuous system. The chain compound may be conductive or dissipative, but the wear strips, supporting structure, grounding path, contamination, and contact between components also matter. Commercial conductive POM chain guidance specifically pairs the material with conductive guide components. Electrical resistance should be measured on the intended configuration using an appropriate electrostatic test method. [Habasit](https://uattsthbst.habasit.com/-/media/Project/Habasit/PublicWebSite/Documents/English/Products/HabaCHAIN/web-4185-en_0416-new-layout.pdf)
8. What should be tested before approving a new chain material?
Start with material and molded-link properties, then test assembled chains. Measure critical dimensions and pitch, hinge articulation, tensile and creep behavior, friction against the actual wear-strip material, pin/bore wear, temperature rise, sprocket engagement, and drive load. Include chemicals, water, debris, lubrication, curves, accumulation, and stop/start cycles where relevant. Samples should ultimately be tested under production-representative conveyor conditions.

Conveyor Chain Components: Material Selection Guide
Conveyor Material Solutions
Plastic conveyor chain components operate under combined tensile loading, hinge articulation, sliding contact, repeated sprocket engagement, and environmental exposure. PLATFORM modified POM compounds can be evaluated where wear, friction, dimensional stability, stiffness, or static-control requirements need to be balanced.
The appropriate material direction depends on the complete conveyor system—not only on the molded chain link.
Conveyor Chain Performance Starts with the System
Plastic conveyor chains are used to move containers, packaged goods, workpieces, electronic components, and industrial products through automated production systems.
Typical molded components include flat-top links, side-flexing links, hinge sections, conveying flights, attachments, retention tabs, and snap-on plates. Depending on the conveyor, these components may carry tensile load, articulate around pins, slide over guide tracks, move through curves, and repeatedly engage sprockets.
These functions create different stresses within the same molded part.
A material that performs well on a straight, lightly loaded conveyor may not perform the same way on a high-speed side-flexing line or a conveyor exposed to abrasive debris and frequent washdown.
Define Chain Pull Before Selecting the Material
The chain must transmit the force required to move both the conveyor and the conveyed product.
Product weight, sliding friction, conveyor length, elevation changes, acceleration, accumulation, and curve resistance can all contribute to chain pull. Startup and jam conditions can produce loads substantially different from normal steady conveying.
Provide the conveyor layout, loaded length, product mass, speed, and operating cycle before evaluating a compound.
If the existing chain is cracking or deforming, determine whether the failure originates from insufficient material capability, excessive chain pull, impact loading, or the geometry of the link.
Hinge Wear Changes the Effective Chain Pitch
The connection between adjacent links is one of the most important wear locations in an articulated conveyor chain.
As the pin and hinge surfaces move relative to each other, wear increases joint clearance. Across many links, these small changes accumulate and appear as chain elongation.
The result can be poor sprocket engagement, noise, vibration, uneven motion, or eventual chain jumping.
For this reason, hinge wear should be measured together with accumulated chain pitch. Replacing a material solely because the chain has become longer can miss problems with pin material, lubrication, chain pull, or sprocket condition.
The Wear Strip Is Part of the Tribological System
Many plastic chains slide continuously over polymer wear strips and guide tracks.
The resulting friction contributes directly to conveyor drive load and interface temperature. Side-flexing chains add another sliding interface where the chain reacts against the corner track.
A useful material evaluation must therefore identify the wear-strip and corner-track materials.
Rough surfaces, embedded abrasive particles, damaged tracks, sharp edges, or incorrect guide clearances can generate plastic dust and rapid wear even when the chain material itself is suitable.
Before changing resin, inspect where the wear debris is actually being generated.
Dry Running Requires More Than a Low-Friction Polymer
A low-friction POM direction may be considered when a conveyor operates dry and friction, heat generation, drive load, or wear has become limiting.
This does not make low-friction POM an automatic choice for every conveyor.
Chain speed, load, guide material, curve geometry, surface condition, contamination, and temperature determine the real tribological system. A material that produces lower friction in one laboratory test may not provide the longest chain life in another conveyor configuration.
Production-intent chain and guide testing remains necessary.
Candidate Material Directions
Unfilled POM
Unfilled POM can be considered as a baseline direction for many molded chain components requiring a balance of strength, stiffness, dimensional stability, moldability, and sliding behavior.
It is particularly relevant when operating conditions are moderate and no special electrical or severe environmental requirement dominates the application.
Validation should include chain pull, hinge durability, dimensions, track wear, sprocket engagement, and service temperature.
Wear-Resistant or Low-Friction POM
A modified tribological POM may be considered for high-cycle, dry-running, higher-speed, or side-flexing conveyors.
The objective may be lower chain/guide friction, reduced plastic dust, lower interface temperature, or improved wear life.
The compound should be tested against the actual wear-strip and corner-track material. Changing only the chain polymer while leaving a rough, contaminated, or damaged guide surface unchanged may not solve the failure.
Reinforced POM
Reinforced POM may be evaluated when structural stiffness, creep, or load-induced deformation becomes limiting.
The tradeoff is more complex.
Fiber orientation creates directional shrinkage and may affect impact behavior, hinge durability, and wear against pins or guides. For precision chain links, improved stiffness is useful only if pitch, articulation, and counterface wear remain acceptable.
A reinforced compound should therefore be treated as a separate material system rather than a direct drop-in replacement for unfilled POM.
Conductive or Antistatic Compounds
Conductive or static-dissipative materials may be considered when the conveyor handles electronic devices or other products with a defined electrostatic-control requirement.
The chain alone is not the ESD system.
Guides, wear strips, the conveyor frame, grounding points, contamination, and electrical continuity must provide a complete charge-dissipation path. Electrical resistance should be validated together with mechanical wear and conveyor performance.
When PA6, PA66, or PPA May Be Relevant
POM should not be forced into every conveyor application.
PA6 or PA66 may deserve evaluation in selected dry abrasive or mechanically demanding environments where a different balance of toughness and wear behavior is required.
Their greater moisture sensitivity must be considered because dimensional change can alter pitch, hinge clearance, and guide interaction.
PPA may be evaluated for projects where elevated-temperature stiffness or creep resistance becomes more important. This direction requires separate assessment of molding conditions, hinge wear, dimensional behavior, economics, and actual service environment.
Temperature and Chemicals Can Change the Material Decision
Conveyor chains may be exposed to water, oil, product residue, cleaners, sanitizers, and other process chemicals.
Do not classify an environment simply as “wet.”
Provide the chemical name, concentration, temperature, exposure duration, and cleaning frequency. A polymer that performs well in water or mild cleaning conditions may not tolerate aggressive oxidizers, concentrated acids, or repeated high-temperature sanitation.
Material-level chemical testing can be used for screening, but stressed molded parts and assembled chains should also be evaluated where exposure is critical.
Injection Molding Controls Chain Geometry
Protect the Hinge Region
The hinge is both a load-bearing feature and a moving bearing surface.
Bore roundness, barrel thickness, pin clearance, and alignment between adjacent hinge sections should be treated as critical functional dimensions.
Weld lines, ejector effects, or excessive flash should not be allowed to compromise these regions.
Control Link Pitch
One molded-link pitch error appears small. Hundreds of links make it a system problem.
Tool shrinkage compensation, mold temperature, packing, cavity balance, and post-molding dimensional change all influence the final pitch.
Critical pitch dimensions should be measured using production material rather than relying on a generic shrinkage value.
Control Flatness
Warped chain links can contact guide tracks unevenly or disturb the stability of conveyed products.
Wall thickness, ribs, hinge geometry, gate location, and cooling should therefore be reviewed for both mechanical function and molded flatness.
Monitor Multi-Cavity Variation
High-volume links are commonly suited to multi-cavity production, but that increases the need for cavity-level dimensional control.
Pitch, hinge bores, attachment geometry, tab thickness, and flatness should be compared by cavity. Representative chain sections should then be assembled and tested for articulation and sprocket engagement.
Common Problems Require Failure Analysis
Increasing chain length: inspect pin and hinge wear, chain pull, creep, and sprocket condition.
Plastic dust or shavings: inspect wear strips, corner tracks, abrasive contamination, speed, lubrication, and guide clearance.
Broken tabs or links: investigate impact, product jams, chemical exposure, local geometry, and load.
High motor current: check chain friction, guide condition, contamination, alignment, and excessive tension.
Rapid curve wear: review chain pull, curve geometry, side-thrust surfaces, speed, and corner-track material.
Static problems: verify the complete electrical path rather than changing only the chain compound.
The material may be part of the solution, but it should not be used to hide a mechanical or conveyor-design problem.
Information Needed for Material Evaluation
Prepare:
Chain link drawing and 3D model
Chain type and pitch
Current material
Pin material and dimensions
Sprocket information
Wear-strip and corner-track materials
Product and conveyor load
Conveyor speed
Straight and curved layout
Dry or lubricated condition
Operating and cleaning temperatures
Chemical and contamination exposure
Existing wear or failure symptoms
Expected service life and production volume
These inputs allow unsuitable material directions to be rejected before tooling or endurance testing.
Validate the Assembled Chain
Material data are useful for initial screening, but the final decision should be made using molded components.
Measure link dimensions, accumulated pitch, hinge clearance, tensile and creep behavior, friction, wear, and dimensional stability.
Then assemble the production-intent chain with the actual pins, sprockets, wear strips, and guide tracks. Run it under representative load, speed, curves, lubrication, temperature, contamination, accumulation, and cleaning conditions.
Track chain elongation, wear debris, drive load, temperature, sprocket engagement, and failure location throughout the test.
