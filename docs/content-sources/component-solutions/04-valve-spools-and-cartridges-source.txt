3. Component Scope
Valve spools and cartridges include internal components that translate, guide, meter, redirect, or shut off fluid flow inside a valve assembly. Depending on the design, the molded component may slide against a bore, carry seals, locate springs, form flow passages, or retain other valve elements. Fluid-power standards treat valve components as part of complete hydraulic or pneumatic systems, where reliable operation, adjustment, maintenance, and safety must be considered together. Molded POM can be relevant to selected valve internals, but high-pressure hydraulic spool and screw-in cartridge functions should not be assumed suitable without application-specific engineering validation. 4. Engineering Priorities
4.1 Pressure and Pressure Differential
Why it matters:
Valve components experience more than static system pressure. Pressure differential across lands, ports, seals, or metering edges can create axial and radial forces on a moving spool. Research on spool valves shows that fluid-dynamic force affects spool equilibrium and response. Where pressure becomes high enough to demand metal-level stiffness, surface finish, or pressure containment, molded POM may no longer be an appropriate direction. [Experts@Minnesota](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/a-modeling-approach-to-study-the-fluid-dynamic-forces-acting-on-t/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Information the customer should provide:
Normal and peak pressure
Maximum differential pressure
Flow direction
Pressure surge or pulsation conditions
Possible validation method:
Pressure-cycle testing, actuation-force measurement, flow testing, and leak testing at temperature.
Risks if ignored:
Slow switching, deformation, leakage, unstable flow, or structural failure.
4.2 Spool-to-Bore Clearance and Concentricity
Why it matters:
A sliding spool depends on controlled radial clearance. Too little clearance can cause sticking when molding variation, thermal expansion, contamination, or pressure distortion is introduced. Too much clearance may increase bypass leakage or reduce metering accuracy. The correct dimension is therefore a functional tolerance between mating parts, not simply a nominal molded diameter.
Information the customer should provide:
Spool OD and mating bore ID
Roundness and cylindricity requirements
Required functional clearance
Maximum allowable leakage
Possible validation method:
CMM or air-gage dimensional inspection followed by hot/cold actuation and leakage testing.
Risks if ignored:
Stiction, slow return, internal leakage, hysteresis, or inconsistent valve response.
4.3 Fluid Compatibility
Why it matters:
The word “fluid” is too vague for material selection. POM has useful resistance to many oils, fuels, water-based media, and common chemicals, but formal POM technical literature also identifies strong acids and oxidizing agents as important limitations. Chemical resistance varies with concentration, temperature, exposure duration, stress, and the specific formulation. [Celanese Explorer](https://explore.celanese.com/rs/139-DBZ-834/images/POM-Hostaform-Product-Manual.pdf?version=0&utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Information the customer should provide:
Exact fluid or chemical name
Concentration
Fluid temperature
Continuous versus intermittent exposure
Possible validation method:
ISO 175 immersion screening followed by testing of stressed molded parts in the real medium. [ISO](https://www.iso.org/standard/55483.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Risks if ignored:
Embrittlement, surface degradation, dimensional change, loss of strength, or leakage.
4.4 Seal Interaction and Actuation Friction
Why it matters:
Many valve spools do not slide directly against the housing alone; they interact with NBR, FKM, EPDM, or other seals. Seal squeeze, groove geometry, lubrication, surface finish, pressure, and polymer stiffness all contribute to actuation force. Research on pneumatic spool-valve seals confirms that sealing pressure, lubrication, geometry, tolerances, and counterface conditions need to be evaluated together. [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232869278_Performance_and_Behavior_of_Seals_for_Pneumatic_Spool_Valves?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Information the customer should provide:
Seal material and geometry
Dry or lubricated operation
Required actuation force
Number of operating cycles
Possible validation method:
Instrumented valve cycling with force/displacement measurement and leakage monitoring.
Risks if ignored:
Stick-slip, excessive solenoid force requirement, seal wear, slow switching, or leakage.
4.5 Contamination and Scoring
Why it matters:
Small particles can become trapped between a spool and its bore or between a seal and sliding surface. Controlled studies of hydraulic spool valves show that contamination can increase friction and contribute to spool clamping or sticking. This is fundamentally a system-cleanliness problem as well as a material problem. [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/9/23/5230?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Information the customer should provide:
Fluid cleanliness or filtration level
Particle source
Expected dust or debris
Existing scoring pattern
Possible validation method:
Clean-fluid and contaminated-fluid cycling tests, followed by surface inspection.
Risks if ignored:
Scoring, sticking, elevated friction, leakage, or intermittent valve behavior.
4.6 Temperature and Dimensional Retention
Why it matters:
A valve may operate correctly at room temperature yet bind, leak, or shift its flow behavior after heating. Polymer dimensions, modulus, creep behavior, seal properties, and fluid viscosity all change with temperature. For a close-clearance spool, the full tolerance stack must be evaluated at actual hot and cold conditions rather than only immediately after molding.
Information the customer should provide:
Minimum and maximum fluid temperature
Ambient temperature
Dwell time at temperature
Thermal cycling requirement
Possible validation method:
Hot/cold dimensional measurement, thermal cycling, pressure testing, and actuation-force measurement.
Risks if ignored:
Clearance closure, leakage growth, deformation, slow return, or inconsistent calibration.
4.7 Flow Geometry and Metering Function
Why it matters:
Metering lands, slots, chamfers, or port edges determine the relationship between spool position and flow area. Research on spool valves shows that internal geometry influences fluid forces and valve response. For molded components, flash, edge rounding, shrinkage, gate effects, or dimensional variation at these features can therefore influence functional flow behavior. [Experts@Minnesota](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/a-modeling-approach-to-study-the-fluid-dynamic-forces-acting-on-t/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Information the customer should provide:
Flow-rate range
Required metering characteristic
Critical land and port dimensions
Allowable flow variation
Possible validation method:
Flow-versus-position testing and pressure-drop characterization using production-intent parts.
Risks if ignored:
Incorrect flow rate, poor repeatability, hysteresis, unstable response, or calibration drift.

6. Candidate Material Directions
6.1 Unfilled POM
When it may be considered:
Selected low- to moderate-pressure valve spools, control pistons, guide elements, cartridge cages, and internal moving parts where dimensional stability, low moisture uptake, stiffness, moldability, and sliding behavior are important.
Potential advantages:
POM provides a useful combination of stiffness, dimensional stability, chemical resistance to many common media, inherent sliding behavior, and relatively low creep and water uptake. Commercial pneumatic and valve equipment already uses POM for selected internal pistons and actuating components, demonstrating technical feasibility at the component-family level. [巴斯夫塑料与橡胶](https://plastics-rubber.basf.com/northamerica/en/performance_polymers/products/ultraform?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Important tradeoffs:
POM is not universally chemically resistant and should not be assumed suitable for high-pressure hydraulic metering surfaces or strong oxidizing/acidic media.
Required project validation:
Pressure, leakage, dimensional, fluid-compatibility, wear, and cycling tests.
Situations where it may not be appropriate:
Very high pressure, severe cavitation/erosion, strong acids, strong oxidizers, or metal-level metering tolerances.
6.2 Wear-Resistant or Low-Friction POM
When it may be considered:
Repeated sliding spools or pistons where friction, stick-slip, wear, squeak, or actuation-force stability has been identified as a limiting factor.
Potential advantages:
Tribologically modified POM can be formulated specifically to improve sliding and abrasion behavior. [巴斯夫塑料与橡胶](https://plastics-rubber.basf.com/northamerica/en/performance_polymers/products/ultraform?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Important tradeoffs:
The modifier can change stiffness, shrinkage, surface behavior, seal interaction, and chemical compatibility. A lower material-level coefficient of friction does not prove lower valve actuation force.
Required project validation:
Cycle testing with the actual bore, seals, fluid, pressure, and temperature.
Situations where it may not be appropriate:
When sticking is caused primarily by contamination, tolerance closure, seal geometry, or housing distortion.
6.3 Reinforced POM
When it may be considered:
Cartridge cages, structural carriers, guide bodies, or other components where stiffness and creep resistance matter more than maintaining a smooth precision sliding interface.
Potential advantages:
Reinforcement can increase stiffness and reduce load-induced deformation.
Important tradeoffs:
Fiber orientation creates anisotropic shrinkage and can complicate roundness, concentricity, and sealing geometry. Exposed fibers can also make reinforced POM less attractive for a precision dynamic sealing or spool surface.
Required project validation:
Fiber orientation, shrinkage, leak tightness, mating-surface wear, pressure cycling, and dimensional stability.
Situations where it may not be appropriate:
Precision sliding lands, dynamic seal contact surfaces, or very small features requiring highly isotropic dimensions.
6.4 Conductive or Antistatic POM
When it may be considered:
Only where the fluid-control assembly has a defined electrostatic-discharge requirement—for example, selected fuel, solvent, electronics, or process-fluid systems.
Potential advantages:
Conductive modification may help establish an electrical dissipation path while retaining molded POM geometry.
Important tradeoffs:
Conductive fillers can alter friction, dimensional behavior, surface finish, toughness, and fluid compatibility. Regulatory and system-grounding requirements may dominate the material decision.
Required project validation:
Electrical resistance plus pressure, fluid exposure, wear, and valve-cycle testing.
Situations where it may not be appropriate:
Ordinary water or pneumatic valves without a quantified static-control requirement.
6.5 PA6, PA66, or PPA
When it may be considered:
PA6 or PA66 may be evaluated for cartridge bodies or structural valve components where a different toughness or reinforcement balance is useful. PPA becomes more technically relevant where elevated-temperature stiffness or dimensional retention under hot conditions exceeds what the POM direction can reasonably provide.
Potential advantages:
Reinforced polyamides and PPA can provide high structural stiffness and thermal capability.
Important tradeoffs:
PA6 and PA66 moisture absorption can alter dimensions, which is potentially problematic for spool clearance and metering geometry. PPA generally reduces—but does not eliminate—moisture-related concerns and imposes more demanding processing requirements.
Required project validation:
Hot/wet dimensions, chemical exposure, pressure cycling, seal interaction, and functional flow testing.
Situations where it may not be appropriate:
Tight-clearance moving spools where stable dimensions and low moisture sensitivity dominate.
7. Design, Tooling, and Processing Considerations
7.1 Treat Cylindricity and Concentricity as Functional Requirements
A spool may have several lands, grooves, seal diameters, or guide surfaces on one axis. Controlling only individual diameters is insufficient if those features are eccentric or tapered relative to each other. ISO 20457 permits additional dimensional and geometric specifications where functional requirements demand them, so the drawing should reflect the actual valve fit rather than rely only on general plastic tolerances. [ISO](https://www.iso.org/cms/%20render/live/en/sites/isoorg/contents/data/standard/06/80/68097.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
7.2 Keep Gates Away from Metering and Dynamic Sealing Surfaces
Gate vestige, local packing variation, or flow-induced orientation near a metering land can affect both dimension and surface condition. Gate location should be chosen so critical spool diameters, sealing lands, and sharp flow-control edges are not unnecessarily compromised. If a weld line crosses a thin pressure-loaded feature or seal carrier, its location should be reviewed during DFM.
7.3 Control Core-Pin Deflection in Cartridge Bodies
Long internal bores and thin annular cartridge geometries often require relatively slender cores. Injection pressure can move or deflect an inadequately supported core, creating eccentric wall thickness or a tapered bore. This matters directly when the bore guides a piston or houses a seal. Core support, gate balance, and cavity pressure therefore affect valve function.
7.4 Compensate for Molding and Post-Molding Shrinkage
POM is semi-crystalline. The final dimensions therefore depend on material, melt flow, cavity pressure, mold temperature, geometry, and time after molding. ISO 294-4 distinguishes molding shrinkage and post-molding shrinkage and also distinguishes shrinkage parallel and transverse to flow. Precision valve tooling should be adjusted from measured production-intent parts rather than a single catalog shrinkage number. [ISO](https://www.iso.org/standard/70413.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
7.5 Avoid Fiber Orientation Problems on Precision Features
If reinforced material is evaluated, flow-induced fiber orientation can create different shrinkage around the spool or cartridge circumference. That may improve stiffness while making cylindricity and sealing geometry harder to hold. Reinforcement therefore makes most sense in structural regions, and less sense on surfaces where a smooth, isotropic molded sliding fit is the main requirement.
7.6 Control Flash, Parting Lines, and Port Edges
A small amount of flash that is harmless on an exterior housing can become critical on a valve spool. Flash or mismatch can cut seals, increase actuation force, obstruct a small flow passage, or interfere with spool travel. Metering edges and seal-entry features should therefore be treated as functional molded surfaces and inspected accordingly.
7.7 Validate Cavity-to-Cavity Flow Performance
For multi-cavity molding, dimensional inspection alone may not expose every functional difference. Small variation in land width, port opening, seal diameter, or concentricity can change actuation force or flow calibration. Critical valve components should therefore be tracked by cavity and, where metering accuracy matters, compared through leakage and flow-versus-position testing.

9. Frequently Asked Questions
1. Is POM suitable for valve spools?
For selected pneumatic, water-control, appliance, and low- to moderate-pressure valve components, POM can be a technically credible candidate because of its dimensional stability, stiffness, sliding behavior, low water uptake, and moldability. It should not be generalized to every spool valve. High-pressure hydraulic spools commonly demand metal-level pressure capability, precision, and surface characteristics. Pressure differential, fluid, clearance, sealing method, and required leakage must be defined first. [ISO](https://www.iso.org/standard/40310.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
2. Why does a plastic spool valve start sticking after some time?
Material wear or swelling is possible, but it is only one explanation. Contamination, scoring, inadequate radial clearance, housing distortion, seal friction, misalignment, thermal expansion, and chemical exposure can all increase actuation resistance. Research on hydraulic spools specifically shows that contamination in the spool-to-body clearance can increase friction and promote sticking. Measure the failed assembly before deciding that a lower-friction material is the answer. [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/9/23/5230?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
3. When should low-friction POM be considered?
It is worth considering when repeated valve cycling shows that sliding friction, wear, stick-slip, or actuation-force variation is a genuine limiting mechanism. Test it with the production bore, seals, lubricant or process fluid, pressure, and temperature. If the problem originates from undersized clearance, seal over-compression, contamination, or an eccentric bore, a tribological compound may reduce symptoms without correcting the real failure mechanism.
4. Can POM be used with water and cleaning chemicals?
POM can perform well in many water and aqueous-fluid applications, and it is used commercially in plumbing and fluid-control systems. That does not mean universal chemical resistance. Formal POM technical literature identifies strong acids and oxidizing agents as important limitations. Request the exact cleaner, sanitizer, concentration, temperature, and exposure duration and validate the specific candidate compound rather than approving it based on “water service” alone. [Celanese](https://www.celanese.com/news-and-media/2013/october/copy-of-celanese-demonstrates-benefits?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
5. How tight can a molded valve spool tolerance be?
There is no universal tolerance that can be promised for a molded POM spool. Capability depends on diameter, length, wall distribution, gate, tool construction, cavity count, shrinkage, mold temperature, packing, measurement method, and whether the requirement is diameter, roundness, cylindricity, or concentricity. ISO 20457 provides a molded-plastic tolerance framework, but critical sliding fits should be specified from functional leakage and actuation requirements. [ISO](https://www.iso.org/cms/%20render/live/en/sites/isoorg/contents/data/standard/06/80/68097.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
6. POM or PA66/PPA—which is better for valve cartridges?
POM is often attractive where sliding behavior, moisture stability, and precision dimensions dominate. PA66 may be useful for more structural or reinforced cartridge features, but moisture conditioning can complicate tight clearances. PPA becomes more relevant when elevated-temperature stiffness and structural retention justify the higher material and processing burden. The comparison should be made on the actual cartridge function rather than choosing one polymer family for every component.
7. Can glass-filled POM improve a valve spool?
Usually this needs more skepticism than the simple answer “glass fiber makes it stronger.” Reinforcement can increase stiffness, but it also creates anisotropic shrinkage and may compromise surface behavior, roundness, seal contact, or mating-part wear. Glass-reinforced POM may make more sense for a structural cartridge carrier than for a precision sliding land. The exact failure mode should determine whether reinforcement deserves testing.
8. What testing is required before approving a new valve material?
Begin with dimensional and material screening, chemical compatibility, and molding trials. Then test production-intent parts for actuation force, leakage, pressure cycling, flow-versus-position behavior, thermal cycling, wear, and seal condition using the real process fluid. Where contamination is realistic, include it in endurance testing. Final approval should be based on valve-level performance rather than tensile properties or a generic coefficient of friction.

Valve Spools and Cartridges: Material Selection Guide
Fluid Control Components
Valve spools and cartridge components must move, seal, guide, and meter fluid while maintaining critical dimensions over repeated operating cycles.
PLATFORM modified POM compounds can be evaluated for selected pneumatic, water-control, appliance, actuator, and industrial valve components where dimensional stability, sliding behavior, stiffness, or wear performance are relevant. Pressure, fluid chemistry, temperature, sealing method, and tolerance requirements must define the application boundary before material selection.
Not Every Valve Spool Is a Plastic Application
The term “valve spool” covers a wide range of operating conditions.
High-pressure hydraulic spool valves and screw-in hydraulic cartridges commonly operate in demanding pressure environments with very tight clearances and highly controlled metal surfaces. These applications should not automatically be converted to plastic simply because POM has good sliding properties.
The more realistic POM opportunities are selected control pistons, low- or moderate-pressure spools, guide elements, cartridge cages, seal carriers, flow-control inserts, and similar internal parts used in pneumatic, water, appliance, actuator, and industrial fluid systems.
Commercial pneumatic equipment already demonstrates selective use of POM for internal pistons and control elements while retaining metal for other critical valve functions. This is the right engineering model: choose material by component function, not by the name of the valve assembly.
Pressure Defines the First Material Boundary
A valve component is exposed to pressure forces as well as mechanical actuation.
Pressure differential across lands, ports, seals, and metering features can create forces on the spool. Internal flow geometry also influences the forces required to move or hold the spool in position.
For a material review, provide normal pressure, peak pressure, maximum differential pressure, flow direction, and any surge or pulsation conditions.
If the design requires high structural stiffness, pressure containment, extremely small leakage clearance, or resistance to severe hydraulic flow forces, a molded POM direction may not be technically appropriate.
That decision should be made early rather than after tooling.
Clearance Controls Both Movement and Leakage
A spool must move through its mating bore without excessive friction while maintaining the required sealing or metering performance.
Too little clearance can create sticking when thermal expansion, molding variation, contamination, seal pressure, or housing deformation is introduced.
Too much clearance can increase internal leakage and reduce flow-control accuracy.
For this reason, the drawing should define more than spool diameter. Roundness, cylindricity, concentricity, mating-bore geometry, and the required functional clearance may all matter.
The final tolerance should be derived from valve function rather than from a generic injection-molding tolerance table.
Fluid Chemistry Must Be Defined Precisely
POM offers useful resistance to many common oils, fuels, water-based media, solvents, and industrial fluids, which is one reason it is used in fluid-control applications.
It is not chemically universal.
Strong acids and oxidizing agents are established limitations for conventional POM materials. Chemical resistance also depends on concentration, fluid temperature, exposure duration, and mechanical stress.
Do not submit an application simply as “water valve” or “chemical valve.”
Provide the exact medium, concentration, temperature, cleaning chemistry, and exposure cycle. Material-level immersion tests can help screen candidates, but the final valve should be tested in the real fluid.
Seals Can Dominate Spool Friction
Many valve spools move through elastomer seals rather than operating as an unsealed polymer-on-polymer sliding pair.
Seal material, compression, groove geometry, lubrication, pressure, edge design, and mating-surface finish all contribute to the force required to move the spool.
A low-friction polymer will not correct an excessively compressed seal or a sharp port edge that damages the seal during travel.
When actuation force is a problem, measure force throughout the complete spool stroke and compare it with pressure, temperature, and seal condition.
Candidate Material Directions
Unfilled POM
Unfilled POM can be considered for selected valve pistons, guides, cartridges, and moving components where dimensional stability, stiffness, relatively low moisture uptake, moldability, and sliding behavior are important.
It is a useful baseline material direction, particularly in moderate operating environments.
Approval still requires testing for pressure, leakage, temperature, fluid compatibility, wear, and cycle life.
Wear-Resistant or Low-Friction POM
A tribologically modified POM may be considered when repeated testing identifies friction, wear, stick-slip, or unstable actuation force as a real limitation.
The complete interface must be evaluated.
The mating bore, seal, fluid, lubrication condition, pressure, temperature, and contamination level can all change the result. A compound with a lower laboratory coefficient of friction does not automatically produce a better valve.
Reinforced POM
Reinforced POM may be useful for cartridge cages, structural carriers, guides, or similar parts where stiffness and reduced deformation are more important than maintaining a precision dynamic sliding surface.
It is less automatically attractive for the spool land itself.
Fiber orientation can produce anisotropic shrinkage, affect roundness, and change the behavior of a surface running against a seal or mating bore.
Use reinforcement because the structural failure mode requires it—not simply because a higher modulus appears better on a data sheet.
Conductive or Antistatic Compounds
A conductive or static-dissipative compound should only be considered where the fluid-control system has a defined electrical requirement.
Examples may include selected fuel, solvent, electronics, or process-fluid systems.
The polymer is only one part of the ESD path. Grounding, mating components, fluid conductivity, contamination, and regulatory requirements must also be considered.
PA6, PA66, or PPA
Alternative polyamide directions may be evaluated when the valve component requires a different structural or thermal balance.
PA6 and PA66 can be useful for reinforced structural cartridge components, but their moisture sensitivity requires care where precise clearances are involved.
PPA may become more relevant for higher-temperature structural components where greater retention of stiffness is required.
These materials should not automatically replace POM on a precision sliding spool. Hot/wet dimensions, chemical compatibility, seals, pressure, processing, and cost all require separate validation.
Contamination Is a Valve Problem, Not Just a Material Problem
A small particle entering a close spool-to-bore clearance can dramatically alter friction.
Particles may score the spool, damage the mating bore, interfere with seals, or create intermittent sticking. Research on hydraulic spool valves has demonstrated the relationship between contamination and increased spool friction.
If a valve begins sticking, inspect the surface before changing polymer.
Determine whether the wear marks indicate abrasive particles, misalignment, inadequate clearance, seal damage, or material degradation.
Improved filtration or cleanliness may provide more value than a different compound.
Injection Molding Must Protect the Functional Surfaces
Control Cylindricity and Concentricity
A valve spool can contain multiple lands, grooves, seal surfaces, and metering features along one axis.
Those features may all be within diameter tolerance and still fail functionally if they are eccentric relative to one another.
Functional drawings should therefore control the geometry that actually governs spool movement and leakage.
Keep Gates Away from Critical Lands
Gate position can influence packing, local shrinkage, surface condition, and flow orientation.
Metering lands, dynamic sealing diameters, and critical port edges should not be compromised simply because a gate location is convenient for molding.
Weld lines should also be reviewed if they cross pressure-loaded or thin structural regions.
Control Internal Cores
Cartridge bodies often contain long bores or annular sections.
The core forming these features can move under injection pressure if the tool is not sufficiently supported or balanced. The resulting bore may become eccentric or tapered.
If that bore guides a moving piston, small tooling errors become valve-function errors.
Account for POM Shrinkage
POM is semi-crystalline and its finished dimensions depend on material, geometry, packing, mold temperature, melt-flow direction, and time after molding.
Tool dimensions should therefore be refined from production-intent molding trials rather than from one generic shrinkage number.
Critical valve dimensions should be rechecked after relevant thermal conditioning.
Eliminate Functional Flash
Flash and parting-line mismatch can be serious defects on valve components.
A small projection can damage a seal, interfere with a spool stroke, obstruct a flow passage, or change a metering edge.
These areas require functional inspection, not just cosmetic acceptance.
Investigate the Failure Before Changing Material
Sticking or slow return: check contamination, spool clearance, seal friction, bore geometry, temperature, and pressure forces.
Increasing leakage: inspect seals, spool wear, bore scoring, creep, and actual clearance.
Scoring: identify the particle source, surface finish, alignment, and fluid cleanliness.
Cracking: review pressure, thin sections, weld lines, assembly stress, and chemical exposure.
Variable flow: inspect metering geometry, cavity-to-cavity dimensions, temperature, and spool position.
A compound change is justified only when the failure analysis shows that material behavior is part of the limiting mechanism.

Normal and peak pressure

Operating temperature
Mating bore material
Spool/bore clearance

Required cycle life
Current failure symptoms
These inputs allow unsuitable materials to be rejected before expensive tooling or valve testing begins.
Validate the Valve, Not Just the Resin
Material properties are useful for screening, but final approval should be based on production-intent valve components.
Measure critical diameters, cylindricity, concentricity, and installed clearance. Test actuation force, internal leakage, pressure cycling, flow-versus-position behavior, temperature response, chemical exposure, wear, and seal condition.
Where contamination is part of the real operating environment, include representative contamination in endurance testing.
The goal is not to prove that a POM compound has good properties. The goal is to prove that the complete valve continues to switch, seal, and meter fluid within the required limits.
