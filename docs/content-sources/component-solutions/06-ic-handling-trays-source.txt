3. Component Scope
IC handling trays are precision carriers used to store, transport, identify, and automatically present packaged semiconductor devices during manufacturing, testing, assembly, and logistics. Matrix trays typically arrange components in defined rows and columns so automation equipment can locate each pocket predictably. IEC 60286-5 establishes common dimensions, tolerances, and characteristics for matrix trays used in automatic handling, while JEDEC Publication 95 includes generic shipping and handling matrix-tray design guidance. The material must therefore support not only the component but also ESD protection, tray flatness, pocket accuracy, stacking behavior, process temperature, cleanliness, and repeated handling. 4. Engineering Priorities
4.1 ESD Protection and Electrical Behavior
Why it matters:
Semiconductor devices can be damaged by electrostatic discharge even when no visible mechanical damage occurs. ESD Association guidance states that material-handling containers used with ESDS items should be low charging and dissipative or conductive within an ESD protected area. Packaging requirements also extend beyond a simple material-resistance value and depend on where and how the tray is used. [ESD协会](https://www.esda.org/esd-overview/esd-fundamentals/part-3-basic-esd-control-procedures-and-materials/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Information the customer should provide:
Required ESD standard or customer specification
Conductive versus dissipative requirement
Use inside or outside an EPA
Grounding/contact configuration
Possible validation method:
Electrical resistance and charge-generation testing using the applicable ANSI/ESD or IEC packaging test methods, followed by assembled-tray verification.
Risks if ignored:
Latent IC damage, uncontrolled triboelectric charge, electrostatic attraction, contamination attraction, or rejection by the customer's ESD program.
4.2 Tray Flatness and Warpage
Why it matters:
A matrix tray interfaces with handlers, elevators, grippers, stackers, magazines, vision systems, and pick-and-place equipment. Excessive bow or twist can change component height, interfere with tray transport, or move pockets away from the expected pickup plane. Tray manufacturers therefore treat flatness and dimensional retention as functional characteristics rather than cosmetic appearance. [RH Murphy Co](https://www.rhmurphy.com/products/ic-matrix-trays/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Information the customer should provide:
Tray outline and thickness
Equipment flatness requirement
Handling method
Temperature history
Possible validation method:
Flatness measurement before and after conditioning, stacking, aging, and process-temperature exposure.
Risks if ignored:
Handler jams, vacuum-pick errors, component misregistration, unstable stacking, or process downtime.
4.3 Pocket Geometry and Device Protection
Why it matters:
The pocket must locate the IC without damaging leads, solder balls, exposed pads, package edges, or other sensitive features. Pocket clearance also influences component movement during transport and automated pickup. Matrix-tray manufacturers therefore design pocket geometry around package body dimensions, component height, loading method, and process requirements rather than using one universal cavity. [ePAK](https://www.epak.com/products/matrix-trays/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Information the customer should provide:
Package type and package drawing
Body dimensions and height
Lead, ball, or pad geometry
Allowed component movement
Possible validation method:
Dimensional inspection, drop/vibration handling trials, automated pickup testing, and post-test package inspection.
Risks if ignored:
Lead damage, solder-ball contact, package scratching, device rotation, pickup failure, or components escaping from pockets.
4.4 Process and Bake Temperature
Why it matters:
Not every semiconductor tray is bakeable. Semiconductor moisture-control procedures can require devices to be placed in thermally suitable carriers during drying processes, while tray manufacturers distinguish between bakeable and non-bakeable materials. Material selection must therefore begin with the maximum process temperature and exposure duration rather than assuming any ESD-safe polymer can enter the same thermal process. Number of thermal cycles

Possible validation method:
Loaded and unloaded thermal conditioning followed by flatness, dimensions, stackability, ESD, and mechanical testing.
Risks if ignored:
Tray warpage, pocket displacement, dimensional loss, sticking between trays, or contamination during heating.
4.5 Cleanliness, Particle Generation, and Material Transfer
Why it matters:
Semiconductor handling places more emphasis on contamination than many conventional industrial plastic parts. Fillers, molded flash, wear debris, damaged pocket edges, and material sloughing can introduce particles into automation equipment or onto sensitive packages. Semiconductor tray suppliers specifically distinguish material systems according to particle-generation behavior and warn that conductive filler selection can affect cleanliness. Risks if ignored:
Package contamination, optical inspection failures, dirty tooling, electrical contamination, or increased maintenance.
4.6 Stackability and Automation Interface
Why it matters:
Matrix trays are normally part of a stack and must repeatedly locate relative to neighboring trays and handling equipment. IEC 60286-5 standardizes the common characteristics required for automatic handling, while JEDEC-type trays use defined exterior features so different pocket patterns can remain compatible with automation. 4.7 Dimensional Stability Over Reuse
Why it matters:
Reusable trays experience repeated handling, stacking, cleaning, thermal exposure, and mechanical loading. Pocket pitch or tray outline can remain acceptable initially but drift after repeated cycles. For automatic handling, small dimensional changes can become significant because equipment relies on predictable pocket locations and tray datums.
Information the customer should provide:

Possible validation method:

Risks if ignored:
Progressive automation errors, uncertain tray life, increased rejects, or field mixing of acceptable and distorted trays.

6. Candidate Material Directions
6.1 Conductive or Static-Dissipative POM
When it may be considered:
Selected lower-temperature IC handling trays, fixtures, or component carriers where a POM platform can meet the defined ESD, dimensional, cleanliness, and process requirements.
Potential advantages:
POM provides good rigidity, low moisture uptake, dimensional stability, and injection moldability. Conductive and electrostatic-dissipative POM technology is commercially established at the polymer-family level. [巴斯夫塑料与橡胶](https://plastics-rubber.basf.com/northamerica/en/performance_polymers/industries/pp_electronics_and_electric/products/ultraform?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Important tradeoffs:
Standard unmodified POM is generally electrically insulating; an ESD tray therefore requires a specifically engineered electrical formulation. Conductive additives can influence shrinkage, flow, surface finish, toughness, and cleanliness.
Required project validation:
Part-level electrical resistance, charge behavior, warpage, pocket accuracy, cleanliness, automation compatibility, and process-temperature testing.
Situations where it may not be appropriate:
High-temperature bake trays, ultra-clean applications beyond the validated formulation, or projects whose required ESD behavior cannot be maintained after molding and reuse.
6.2 Dimensionally Controlled or Reinforced POM
When it may be considered:
Large or thin trays where stiffness or warpage becomes limiting and the tray remains within the validated temperature range.
Potential advantages:
Reinforcement may increase rigidity and reduce load-induced deflection.
Important tradeoffs:
Fiber orientation introduces anisotropic shrinkage, which can be particularly problematic in a long, flat precision tray. Reinforcement may also affect surface cleanliness and pocket-contact behavior.
Required project validation:
Mold-flow orientation, longitudinal and transverse shrinkage, tray flatness, ESD performance, particle generation, and package-contact inspection.
Situations where it may not be appropriate:
Trays requiring extremely clean pocket surfaces or geometries where fiber-driven distortion cannot be adequately controlled.
6.3 Unfilled POM for Non-ESDS Handling Components
When it may be considered:
Mechanical tray frames, fixtures, dividers, alignment pieces, or carriers that do not directly serve as ESD protective packaging for sensitive semiconductor devices.
Potential advantages:
Good dimensional stability, low moisture uptake, stiffness, and moldability.
Important tradeoffs:
Most conventional POM grades have high electrical resistance and should not be assumed suitable for ESDS packaging. Celanese technical literature, for example, distinguishes ordinary POM from specially antistatic or conductive formulations. [Celanese](https://www.celanese.com/en/-/media/Engineered%20Materials/Files/Product%20Technical%20Guides/POM-065-HostaformPOMEU-PM-EN-r1-0916.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Required project validation:
Electrical-risk assessment, dimensional testing, cleanliness, and equipment compatibility.
Situations where it may not be appropriate:
Direct ESDS packaging or handling where dissipative/conductive behavior is required.
6.4 PPA for Higher-Temperature Requirements
When it may be considered:
When process temperature or thermal dimensional stability exceeds the practical validated range of the available POM direction and a higher-temperature injection-molded material is required.
Potential advantages:
PPA may provide better retention of stiffness at elevated temperature and lower moisture sensitivity than conventional PA6 or PA66.
Important tradeoffs:
PPA does not automatically qualify as an IC tray material. ESD modification, warpage, cleanliness, pocket wear, molding conditions, and cost still require validation.
Required project validation:
Thermal cycling, bake exposure, electrical performance, dimensional stability, contamination, stackability, and automated handling.
Situations where it may not be appropriate:
Where the required temperature is modest and a validated POM direction provides simpler processing or better economics.
6.5 Other High-Temperature Tray Materials
When it may be considered:
If baking, burn-in, or other process temperatures exceed the validated performance window of both POM and the available PPA direction.
Potential advantages:
Established semiconductor tray manufacturers use dedicated high-temperature polymer systems for demanding thermal processes. [RH Murphy Co](https://www.rhmurphy.com/products/esd-trays/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Important tradeoffs:
This may fall outside Taiyi Polymer’s current core POM positioning and requires project-specific material sourcing and qualification.
Required project validation:
Full thermal, ESD, contamination, dimensional, and semiconductor-process qualification.
Situations where it may not be appropriate:
Routine room-temperature handling where the additional material and processing cost provides no useful benefit.
7. Design, Tooling, and Processing Considerations
7.1 Design the Tray for Flatness, Not Just Strength
A matrix tray is a large, thin precision molding. Increasing local thickness without controlling the full rib structure can create differential shrinkage rather than solving warpage. Outer frame, transverse ribs, longitudinal ribs, pocket walls, and stacking features should be balanced as one structure. Flatness should be checked after molding, conditioning, stacking, and the highest intended process temperature.
7.2 Protect Pocket Pitch and Automation Datums
Automation does not care only whether each IC fits its pocket. It also expects the pocket matrix to remain in a predictable relationship to tray datums and orientation features. IEC 60286-5 focuses specifically on dimensions required for handling and component placement/removal. Pocket pitch, first-pocket position, tray outline, and automation locating features should therefore be treated as one tolerance system. [IEC Webstore](https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/63037?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
7.3 Control Pocket Contact with the Package
A pocket should retain the IC through handling without placing unnecessary load on leads, balls, pads, or fragile package edges. Draft angle, corner relief, support height, bottom openings, and retention features must be reviewed against the actual package drawing. Ejector marks, flash, and gate residue should be kept away from sensitive device-contact regions.
7.4 Balance Gate Location and Melt Flow
Long-flow tray geometries can produce orientation differences across the part. With conductive or reinforced compounds, this becomes more important because filler orientation can influence shrinkage, warpage, mechanical behavior, and even molded electrical performance. Gate system design should therefore be evaluated with flow simulation and real molded-tray measurement rather than using a generic resin shrinkage value.
7.5 Treat ESD Performance as a Molded-Part Property
Conductive filler networks can respond to wall thickness, flow orientation, shear, molding temperature, and other processing variables. POM technical literature explicitly notes that molded-part electrical properties can depend on processing and part design. Measure the actual tray at defined locations rather than qualifying the resin pellet data alone. [Celanese](https://www.celanese.com/en/-/media/Engineered%20Materials/Files/Product%20Technical%20Guides/POM-065-HostaformPOMEU-PM-EN-r1-0916.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
7.6 Control Stack Features After Thermal Conditioning
Stacking rails, tabs, stops, and nesting surfaces can remain correct at room temperature yet interfere after heat exposure or repeated reuse. Testing should include complete stacks, not only individual trays. Evaluate separation force, stack height, lateral stability, and compatibility with magazines or automated de-stackers after the intended conditioning cycle.
7.7 Minimize Flash, Debris, and Surface Damage
A semiconductor tray is a material-handling product, but its cleanliness requirements can resemble those of a process component. Flash at pocket edges, broken ribs, abrasive trimming, gate debris, or filler sloughing can contaminate devices and equipment. Tool maintenance, gate trimming, handling, cleaning, and packaging after molding should therefore be included in the process-control plan.

9. Frequently Asked Questions
1. Is POM suitable for IC handling trays?
It may be considered for selected lower-temperature trays or handling components, but standard POM should not be treated as the default semiconductor tray material. ESDS trays normally require defined conductive or dissipative behavior, while conventional POM is typically electrically insulating. The tray must also satisfy flatness, pocket accuracy, cleanliness, process-temperature, and automation requirements. A specifically modified POM direction therefore requires tray-level qualification rather than approval based on base-polymer properties. [Celanese](https://www.celanese.com/en/-/media/Engineered%20Materials/Files/Product%20Technical%20Guides/POM-065-HostaformPOMEU-PM-EN-r1-0916.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
2. Do IC trays need to be conductive?
Not every tray requires exactly the same electrical classification. The requirement depends on the ESDS item, whether the tray is inside or outside an ESD protected area, the packaging architecture, customer specification, and grounding method. ESD Association guidance describes conductive and static-dissipative handling containers as part of ESD control, while packaging standards define properties based on the intended protective function. Start with the customer's ESD program rather than selecting a resistance value arbitrarily. [ESD协会](https://www.esda.org/esd-overview/esd-fundamentals/part-3-basic-esd-control-procedures-and-materials/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
3. Can a POM tray be used for semiconductor baking?
Do not assume so. A tray intended for baking requires a material and molded design qualified for the actual temperature, duration, loading, and number of cycles. Semiconductor tray manufacturers distinguish bakeable from non-bakeable tray materials, and moisture-sensitive-device handling procedures distinguish thermally suitable carriers from lower-temperature carriers. If baking is required, it should be one of the first material-selection inputs. [RH Murphy Co](https://www.rhmurphy.com/products/jedec-trays/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
4. What causes an IC tray to warp?
Material shrinkage and thermal stiffness matter, but design and molding conditions can be equally important. Large differences between the frame, ribs, and pocket structure; unbalanced filling; filler orientation; uneven cooling; packing differences; thermal exposure; and long-term stacked loading can all contribute. Before switching compounds, measure the warpage pattern and determine whether it follows melt flow, geometry, temperature, or stacking conditions.
5. How tight should IC tray pocket tolerances be?
There is no universal pocket tolerance that should be copied onto every tray. The required clearance depends on package body dimensions, leads or solder balls, pickup method, permitted component movement, tray warpage, molding capability, and automation requirements. IEC 60286-5 standardizes the tray characteristics necessary for automatic handling, while the pocket itself still needs to be designed around the specific component. [IEC Webstore](https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/63037?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
6. POM or PPA—which direction is better for IC trays?
For lower-temperature applications, a properly engineered conductive or dissipative POM may offer useful dimensional stability and molding behavior. PPA becomes more relevant when elevated-temperature stiffness is a major requirement. Neither polymer is automatically qualified for semiconductor trays. The comparison must include ESD properties, flatness, filler cleanliness, processing, pocket accuracy, thermal cycling, and actual handler compatibility—not only heat resistance or tensile modulus.
7. Can carbon-filled material contaminate semiconductor devices?
The filler system and compound quality matter. Semiconductor tray manufacturers explicitly identify particle generation and conductive-filler sloughing as cleanliness concerns. This does not mean every carbon-modified polymer is unacceptable, but it does mean electrical conductivity cannot be evaluated separately from particle behavior. Inspect production-intent molded trays after repeated loading, unloading, rubbing, cleaning, and thermal conditioning before approval. [RH Murphy Co](https://www.rhmurphy.com/resources/5-questions-ask-your-jedec-tray-supplier/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
8. What samples and tests are needed before qualification?
Start with molded trays, not only resin plaques. Measure electrical resistance at multiple locations, tray flatness, critical outline dimensions, pocket pitch, pocket geometry, stacking behavior, and package fit. Then expose trays to the intended temperature, humidity, cleaning, reuse, and automation cycles. Final trials should use the real semiconductor package and production handler to evaluate pickup reliability, device movement, contamination, ESD performance, and tray durability.

IC Handling Trays: Material Selection and Design Guide
Semiconductor Handling
IC handling trays must protect semiconductor devices while maintaining pocket position, tray flatness, stackability, and compatibility with automated equipment.
PLATFORM modified POM compounds may be evaluated for selected lower-temperature handling trays where controlled electrical properties, dimensional stability, and injection molding performance are required. Final material selection must be based on the ESD specification, process temperature, semiconductor package, cleanliness requirement, tray geometry, and qualification results.
IC Trays Are Precision Process Components
Matrix trays are used to transport, store, sort, test, and automatically present semiconductor devices throughout back-end manufacturing and electronics assembly.
Typical applications include BGA, QFN, LGA, QFP, CSP, MEMS, and other packaged components.
The pockets are arranged in a controlled matrix so automated equipment can locate each device repeatedly. International standards therefore address tray dimensions and characteristics required for automatic handling.
This means an IC tray is not simply a molded packaging container.
Its electrical behavior, flatness, pocket geometry, surface condition, process-temperature capability, and dimensional stability can directly affect semiconductor handling reliability.
Define the ESD Requirement First
Semiconductor devices can be sensitive to electrostatic discharge long before any mechanical damage becomes visible.
For ESDS handling, the tray must fit within the customer's ESD-control program. Depending on the application, packaging and handling materials may need low-charging, dissipative, conductive, or shielding-related properties.
Do not specify a tray only as “antistatic.”
Define the applicable ESD standard, required electrical classification, environmental conditioning, tray grounding or contact arrangement, and whether the tray is used inside or outside an ESD protected area.
These requirements determine whether an electrically modified POM direction deserves further evaluation.
Standard POM Is Not Automatically an ESD Material
POM offers several useful mechanical characteristics for precision molded parts, including stiffness, dimensional stability, low moisture uptake, and good injection moldability.
Conventional POM, however, is generally electrically insulating.
For an ESDS tray, a conductive or static-dissipative formulation would therefore need to be developed and qualified specifically for the required electrical behavior.
Conductive and electrostatic-dissipative POM technology exists at the polymer-family level, but electrical modification changes the engineering problem.
Fillers can affect flow, shrinkage, toughness, warpage, surface finish, and cleanliness. The finished molded tray—not the pellet data alone—must be tested.
Tray Flatness Is an Automation Requirement
A semiconductor matrix tray is a relatively large, thin molding.
Small changes in flatness can affect how the tray sits in a magazine, moves through a handler, presents components to a robot, or stacks against neighboring trays.
Warpage can come from the material, but it can also come from tray architecture and molding conditions.
Outer frame thickness, pocket walls, ribs, stacking rails, gate position, filler orientation, packing, mold temperature, and cooling balance all contribute.
Changing to a stiffer material without correcting an unbalanced tray design may reduce one deformation mode while creating another.
Measure the complete molded tray under the actual conditioning and process temperatures.
Pocket Geometry Must Protect the Semiconductor
The pocket has two conflicting tasks.
It must locate the device accurately enough for handling and transport while avoiding harmful contact with leads, solder balls, exposed pads, or sensitive package edges.
Provide the complete semiconductor package drawing rather than only nominal length and width.
The tray designer should understand package height, terminal geometry, pickup surface, orientation requirements, acceptable device movement, and the intended loading and unloading method.
Draft, corner relief, support surfaces, bottom openings, and retention features should be designed around those requirements.
Flash, ejector marks, and gate residue should not interfere with sensitive device-contact areas.
Process Temperature Can Eliminate a Material Early
Not every IC tray is intended for baking.
Moisture-sensitive semiconductor processes may require thermally suitable carriers during drying or other elevated-temperature operations. Tray suppliers therefore offer separate bakeable and non-bakeable material systems.
If the tray will experience elevated temperature, provide the actual temperature, duration, number of cycles, loading condition, and whether the semiconductor remains in the tray.
A material that retains basic strength may still be unsuitable if the tray loses flatness or pocket accuracy.
POM should therefore be treated primarily as a candidate for applications whose validated thermal requirement fits the selected formulation.
Where higher-temperature processing dominates the project, PPA or another dedicated high-temperature polymer direction may require evaluation.
Candidate Material Directions
Conductive or Static-Dissipative POM
This is the most relevant POM direction for trays that directly handle ESD-sensitive semiconductor devices.
Potential advantages include POM's dimensional stability, rigidity, relatively low moisture uptake, and injection molding capability combined with controlled electrical behavior.
The tradeoff is formulation complexity.
Electrical resistance, charge behavior, filler cleanliness, shrinkage, flatness, and pocket dimensions all need to remain stable after molding and repeated use.
This direction requires actual tray qualification.
Dimensionally Controlled or Reinforced POM
Reinforcement may be considered where tray stiffness or long-span deformation becomes limiting.
It is not automatically a better solution.
Fiber orientation can create different shrinkage along and across the tray and can increase warpage if gate and rib architecture are poorly matched to the compound.
Reinforcement can also affect the surface exposed to the semiconductor.
Use it only when tray-level trials show a meaningful dimensional advantage without unacceptable ESD or cleanliness tradeoffs.
Unfilled POM
Unfilled POM can be useful for non-ESDS mechanical fixtures, alignment parts, dividers, or associated handling components.
It should not be presented as a default material for ESD-sensitive IC packaging.
If the component directly handles ESDS devices, the electrical requirement must be verified before unfilled POM is considered acceptable.
PPA
PPA may be considered when a project requires higher thermal performance than the validated POM direction can provide.
Its higher-temperature mechanical capability can be useful, but it still requires appropriate ESD modification and tray-level qualification.
Do not treat PPA as a shortcut to a bakeable semiconductor tray.
Flatness, pocket dimensions, contamination, processing, electrical behavior, and actual thermal-cycle performance must all be demonstrated.
Cleanliness Is Part of Material Selection
Electrical conductivity is not the only semiconductor-specific requirement.
The tray should not become an avoidable source of particles or conductive debris.
Filler sloughing, flash, gate trimming debris, damaged pocket edges, and surface wear can contaminate components and handling equipment.
Where cleanliness is important, request the customer's particle, outgassing, or contamination requirements before selecting the formulation.
If the tray will be reused, cleanliness should also be evaluated after repeated loading, unloading, cleaning, and thermal exposure.
A compound that passes an initial electrical test but generates unacceptable particles during service is not an acceptable tray solution.
Design for the Automation Interface
Control the Pocket Matrix
Automation equipment relies on a defined relationship between tray datums and pocket locations.
The first pocket position, row and column pitch, tray outline, orientation features, and stacking geometry should therefore be treated as a coordinated dimensional system.
A semiconductor package can fit perfectly in every pocket while the tray still fails in the handler because the complete matrix is displaced or warped.
Protect Tray Flatness
A large flat part needs balanced structural design.
Ribs should support stiffness without creating severe local thickness changes or asymmetric shrinkage.
Tray flatness should be measured after molding and again after the environmental and thermal conditions expected in production.
Review Gate and Flow Orientation
Gate position affects how the melt travels through the tray.
With conductive or reinforced compounds, flow orientation can influence both directional shrinkage and filler distribution.
Mold-flow analysis can help identify risk, but production-intent molding and measurement remain necessary.
Control Stacking Features
A tray normally operates as part of a stack.
Locating rails, tabs, edges, and stops must continue to function after thermal conditioning and repeated use.
Validate complete tray stacks in the intended magazines, elevators, de-stackers, and handling equipment.
Common Problems Should Be Diagnosed Before Changing Resin
Tray warpage: review geometry, melt flow, filler orientation, cooling, thermal exposure, and stacking load.
Pickup failures: measure tray flatness, pocket position, device height, and automation calibration.
Component movement: compare actual pocket clearance and support geometry with the package drawing.
ESD failures: verify molded-part resistance, conditioning, grounding, and the complete ESD packaging architecture.
Particle contamination: identify whether debris comes from the compound, flash, gate trimming, damaged pockets, or external handling.
Bake distortion: confirm that the tray material was actually qualified for the specified thermal process.
Changing polymer can be necessary, but only after identifying which requirement the existing system fails.
What Information Should You Provide?
For a useful material evaluation, prepare:
Semiconductor package drawing
Required tray standard or outline
Pocket matrix and pitch
Package dimensions and terminal geometry
ESD specification
Maximum process temperature
Required tray flatness
Automation and magazine interface
Cleaning procedure
Reuse requirement
Cleanliness or contamination specification
Current tray material
Current failure symptoms
Qualification and documentation requirements
These inputs allow unsuitable material directions to be rejected before tooling and semiconductor qualification work begins.
Validate the Molded Tray
Resin properties are only the starting point.
Electrical performance should be measured on the actual molded tray at representative locations. Flatness, pocket dimensions, matrix position, tray outline, stack features, and device fit should also be inspected.
Next, expose the tray to the intended temperature, humidity, cleaning, stacking, and reuse conditions.
Finally, run production-intent trays through the actual semiconductor-handling equipment with representative devices.
Track component pickup, device movement, tray warpage, ESD behavior, contamination, surface damage, and dimensional drift.
The objective is not simply to produce an electrically conductive plastic tray.
The objective is to maintain semiconductor protection and automated handling performance throughout the required process.
